Product design · 2026

ErgoScan

An AR app for iOS that reads your desk setup and turns it into posture fixes you can actually make, with no new equipment required.

Role

UX/UI, testing, build

Team

With Diana Amriyeva

Tools

Figma, Xcode, ARKit

Award

RGD Honorable Mention

The problem

Most posture advice is generic and hard to apply at a real desk. ErgoScan uses AR to measure the things that actually affect posture, like monitor eye level and the distance between your keyboard and the desk edge, then has AI translate that into small, specific adjustments.

Design process

I owned the design side end to end. I designed the app from lo-fi wireframes through hi-fi UI, including a traffic light color system for feedback (green, yellow, red) chosen because it needs no explanation. I then built the front-end prototype myself in Xcode, using Claude to help with the SwiftUI and ARKit implementation. Diana led the concept, the UX flow, and the backend, including the OpenAI API integration.

The app

Testing and iteration

I ran user testing in two rounds: informally with classmates during the course, then more broadly with around 20 visitors at our year-end show, using a survey. Both rounds fed directly back into the UI.

Impact

At the show, visitors told us this wasn't just a school project. It was something they'd actually use at their own desk. That reaction was the clearest signal that the problem and the fix were both real.

Honorable Mention, RGD Student Awards 2026, recognized alongside the show feedback as external validation of the project.

UX research · 2025

Stu-View usability test

A moderated usability study of GBC's Stu-View student portal, evaluating how well students complete common campus tasks.

Full write-up coming soon. The study ran with 5 Interaction Design students across 4 tasks (student awards, payment plan, photo ID reissue, class schedule). Task 2 had the most critical errors despite the shortest time, pointing to a misleading UI path. Key findings: card-based layout was hard to scan, top-level categories were ambiguous, and 3 out of 5 participants abandoned navigation and searched instead. Email me if you'd like the full report early.
